The Sentence After a Slip

The short version

One unplanned meal is an event. It is not a verdict about your character. The sentence “I blew it, so I will start Monday” can convert one decision into fifteen more. Replace it with: That was one meal. The next one is mine. Do not invent a punishment. Return to the next ordinary meal you planned.

I know the arithmetic because I have lived it. Tuesday night, one sleeve of cookies. Then the sentence arrives: I blew it. I will start again Monday.

Wednesday, Thursday, Friday, Saturday, Sunday. Five days. Three meals a day. One unplanned meal becomes fifteen more decisions surrendered because Monday sounds cleaner than Wednesday morning.

I call it Monday Math, and the math is not the worst part. The worst part is that the sentence feels like accountability while it is actually relief. If the whole week is already lost, I do not have to fight for the next meal.

What is the sentence after a slip?

It is the story you tell yourself immediately after the event. “The cookies were there, I was tired, and I made one decision” is information. “I have no discipline, I always fail, and none of this matters until Monday” is character assassination followed by a permission slip.

Specific facts can teach you something. Identity verdicts shut the investigation down. Twenty-three years as a cop taught me the difference between an explanation and an acquittal. A report can explain every circumstance and still leave responsibility exactly where it belongs. Recovery works the same way. We learn the trigger without pretending the next choice has disappeared.

That was one meal. The next one is mine.

That sentence does not promise perfection. It does something better. It returns jurisdiction to the next controllable decision.

What is the abstinence violation effect?

Behavioral researchers use the term for a pattern where guilt and self-blame after a lapse increase the chance of abandoning the larger change. The lapse becomes proof that the person is broken. Once that belief takes hold, returning to the behavior starts to feel inevitable.

The useful distinction is between a lapse and a relapse. A lapse is one episode. A relapse is the larger return to the pattern. The first does not legally require the second, no matter how persuasive the voice sounds at midnight.

Should I fast to make up for overeating?

Do not turn fasting into a punishment invented while ashamed. That keeps food at the center of a debt-and-payment system. The recovery move described here is deliberately ordinary: return to the next meal you already planned, at the time you planned it.

If fasting is part of your life, decide on it calmly, with judgment and with appropriate medical guidance. A planned fasting practice and a panicked attempt to erase a meal are not the same thing.

What should I do immediately after a slip?

First, close the episode. Say the sentence out loud: “That was one meal. The next one is mine.”

Second, describe what happened without attacking your identity. Where were you? What time was it? Were you tired, alone, angry, bored, or standing next to the food? What decision point appeared before the first bite?

Third, return to the next ordinary meal already in the plan. Do not wait for a symbolic morning, month, birthday, or Monday. The next bite is the only decision that exists.

Fourth, change one part of the environment. Move the trigger food. Leave the kitchen. Prepare the planned meal. Text the person who knows what you are working on. Make the good choice easier before motivation has to carry it.

What if this keeps happening?

There is a difference between an occasional slip and recurring episodes involving loss of control, distress, secrecy, or binge eating. If that is the pattern, calling a qualified medical or mental-health professional is the behavioral equivalent of calling for backup. It is disciplined, not weak.

This show is not a purity contest. I once carried 365 pounds, reached 231, and was around 280 in this recording. I am not shouting from a finish line. I am working the same next decision from inside the struggle.

Common questions

Did one bad meal erase my progress?

No. One meal is one event. The danger is converting it into permission to abandon the next decision.

What sentence should I use?

“That was one meal. The next one is mine.” Put it on the refrigerator, pantry, mirror, or phone before the next difficult night.

What meal should come next?

The ordinary meal you calmly planned in advance, not a reward and not a punishment.
